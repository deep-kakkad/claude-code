What is STP Marketing?

STP is the strategic foundation of modern marketing. It's how brands identify who to serve, which customers to focus on, and how to stand out. Every successful company—from Apple to Airbnb—uses this framework.

S

Segmentation

Divide the market into distinct groups of customers with similar needs, characteristics, or behaviors. Think demographics, psychographics, behavior, and geography.

Example: Nike segments by sport type (runners, basketball, soccer), skill level (amateur vs. pro), and lifestyle (athleisure)

T

Targeting

Evaluate each segment and decide which ones to pursue. You can't serve everyone. Choose segments where you can win and deliver the most value.

Example: Tesla targets environmentally-conscious, tech-savvy, affluent customers who want luxury + sustainability

P

Positioning

Create a unique place in the customer's mind. How are you different? Why should they choose you over competitors? This becomes your brand identity.

Example: Volvo = Safety. Apple = Innovation + Simplicity. Walmart = Low Prices

💡 Why STP Changed Everything

Before STP, companies used "mass marketing"—one message for everyone. In the 1950s, Wendell Smith realized this was wasteful. Different customers want different things. By segmenting markets, targeting specific groups, and positioning uniquely, companies could serve customers better and compete more effectively.

Today, with digital marketing and data analytics, STP is more powerful than ever. You can segment with precision, target microscopically, and position dynamically.

— Wendell Smith, Marketing Pioneer

Segmentation: Divide to Conquer

Your market is not homogeneous. Customers have different needs, budgets, values, and behaviors. Segmentation identifies meaningful groups you can serve distinctly.

The Four Types of Segmentation

👥
Demographic
Age, gender, income, education, occupation, family size. The most common and accessible data.
Examples: Gen Z, High-income professionals, Parents
🌍
Geographic
Country, region, city, climate, urban vs. rural. Location shapes needs and preferences.
Examples: Urban millennials, Coastal cities, Tropical regions
🧠
Psychographic
Lifestyle, values, personality, interests, attitudes. The "why" behind behavior.
Examples: Eco-conscious, Adventure seekers, Status-driven
📊
Behavioral
Purchase patterns, usage rate, brand loyalty, benefits sought. What people actually do.
Examples: Heavy users, Price-sensitive, First-time buyers

🎯 Apple's Segmentation Genius

Demographic: Affluent consumers (premium pricing)

Psychographic: Creative professionals, design-conscious, value simplicity and aesthetics

Behavioral: Early adopters, high engagement, ecosystem lock-in (iPhone → Mac → iPad)

Notice how Apple combines multiple segmentation types. They don't just target "rich people"—they target rich people who value design, creativity, and seamless experiences. This multi-dimensional segmentation creates laser focus.

— Tim Cook, Apple CEO

🎯 Practice Exercise: Segment Your Market
  1. Choose a product or service (your business or one you admire)
  2. List 3-5 distinct customer groups who might buy it
  3. Describe each group using demographic, geographic, psychographic, and behavioral factors
  4. Identify which segments are most attractive (size, growth, profitability)
  5. Notice how each segment might want different features or messaging

Targeting: Choose Your Battlefield

You've identified segments. Now: which ones should you serve? You can't be everything to everyone. Strategic targeting means choosing where you can win.

The Four Targeting Strategies

🌐
Undifferentiated (Mass)
Same product/message for everyone. Economies of scale, but no customization.
Example: Coca-Cola (same formula everywhere)
🎯
Differentiated (Multi-Segment)
Different offerings for different segments. More market coverage, higher costs.
Example: Marriott (luxury, business, budget hotels)
🔬
Concentrated (Niche)
Focus all resources on one segment. Deep expertise, but higher risk.
Example: Rolex (luxury watch buyers only)
👤
Micromarketing (1-to-1)
Customize for individuals or local markets. Maximum relevance, hard to scale.
Example: Netflix (personalized recommendations)

How to Evaluate Segments

Criteria What to Look For Why It Matters
📏 Size Is the segment large enough to be profitable? Too small = wasted effort. Too large = might not be distinct.
📈 Growth Is it growing, stable, or declining? Growth segments offer future opportunity.
🎯 Accessibility Can you reach them effectively? Need distribution channels and communication paths.
💰 Profitability Will serving them be profitable? High acquisition costs or low margins kill returns.
🔧 Fit Does it align with your capabilities and brand? Play to your strengths. Don't chase mismatches.
🛡️ Defensibility Can you build a sustainable competitive advantage? If competitors can easily copy, margins compress.

💡 Tesla's Targeting Evolution

Phase 1 (Roadster): Ultra-rich early adopters. Tiny segment, but proved electric could be sexy.

Phase 2 (Model S/X): Affluent, environmentally-conscious, tech-forward buyers. Niche but growing.

Phase 3 (Model 3/Y): Upper-middle class. Multi-segment targeting while maintaining premium positioning.

Tesla started concentrated (niche) and expanded to differentiated (multi-segment) as they scaled. This is the classic startup playbook: dominate a niche, then expand.

— Elon Musk, Tesla CEO

Positioning: Own a Word in the Mind

Positioning is how customers perceive you relative to competitors. It's the mental real estate you own. When someone says "safety," you think Volvo. "Luxury," you think Rolex. That's positioning.

The Positioning Statement Formula

For [target customer] who [need/want],
[Brand name] is a [category]
that [point of difference].
Unlike [competitors],
we [reason to believe].

Real Examples:

🍎 Apple

For creative professionals and design-conscious consumers who value simplicity and premium experiences, Apple is a technology company that creates beautifully designed, intuitive products that just work. Unlike PC manufacturers, we control the entire ecosystem from hardware to software.

✔️ Nike

For athletes and active individuals who want to perform at their best, Nike is a athletic footwear and apparel brand that delivers innovative, performance-enhancing products. Unlike lifestyle brands, we partner with the world's greatest athletes and use cutting-edge technology.

🚗 Volvo

For safety-conscious families who prioritize protection over flashiness, Volvo is an automobile manufacturer that builds the safest cars on the road. Unlike luxury brands focused on status, we pioneered the three-point seatbelt and countless safety innovations.

🧠 The Laws of Positioning (Al Ries & Jack Trout)

1. Be First: It's better to be first in the mind than best. Coca-Cola, Kleenex, Google—they own categories.

2. If You Can't Be First, Create a New Category: Can't beat Coca-Cola? Red Bull created "energy drinks."

3. Own One Word: Volvo = Safety. FedEx = Overnight. BMW = Driving. What's your word?

4. The Power of Narrow Focus: Specialists beat generalists. Domino's: "Pizza delivery in 30 minutes."

5. Repositioning the Competition: Don't just position yourself—reposition competitors. Avis: "We're #2, so we try harder."

— Al Ries & Jack Trout, "Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind"

STP in Action: Real Brand Examples

See how world-class brands use STP to dominate their markets. Study these examples to understand how strategy translates to success.

A
Airbnb
"Belong Anywhere"
Segmentation
Millennials seeking authentic experiences, budget travelers, families wanting space, digital nomads needing monthly stays
Targeting
Experience-driven travelers (vs. traditional hotel guests) who value local culture and unique stays over standardized service
Positioning
Not a hotel—a way to "live like a local." Authentic, unique accommodations that create belonging, not transactions
N
Netflix
"See What's Next"
Segmentation
Binge watchers, cord-cutters, families, international viewers, niche content fans (anime, documentaries, etc.)
Targeting
Micromarketing: personalized content for each user based on viewing history, preferences, and behavior patterns
Positioning
Personalized entertainment on-demand. Watch what you want, when you want, with algorithmic recommendations that know you better than you know yourself
P
Peloton
"Together We Go Far"
Segmentation
Affluent professionals (time-poor), fitness enthusiasts, parents who can't go to gyms, tech-savvy exercisers
Targeting
Upper-middle-class individuals willing to pay premium ($2000+ bike + $44/month) for at-home boutique fitness experience
Positioning
Not a bike—a connected fitness platform. Studio-quality classes at home with community, gamification, and world-class instructors
S
Spotify
"Music for Everyone"
Segmentation
Music lovers, podcast listeners, commuters, workout enthusiasts, free tier users, premium subscribers
Targeting
Multi-segment: freemium model serves ad-tolerant users while converting heavy listeners to premium subscriptions
Positioning
Personal soundtrack to your life. Algorithmic discovery + massive library + personalized playlists (Discover Weekly changed music discovery)
S
Stripe
"Payments Infrastructure for the Internet"
Segmentation
Developers, startups, SaaS companies, online marketplaces, platforms needing payment processing
Targeting
Developer-first companies and startups that value API simplicity and fast integration over traditional sales relationships
Positioning
Not just payment processing—the economic infrastructure of the internet. Seven lines of code to start accepting payments globally
L
Lululemon
"Sweat Life"
Segmentation
Yoga enthusiasts, athleisure consumers, health-conscious women 24-45, community-oriented fitness lovers
Targeting
Affluent, active women who see fitness as lifestyle (not just exercise) and value premium quality + community
Positioning
Premium yoga-inspired athletic wear that's both performance-focused and stylish. $100 yoga pants aren't crazy—they're an investment in your practice

🔍 Pattern Recognition: What Winners Do Differently

1. Hyper-Specific Segmentation: Notice how none of these brands target "everyone." They find narrow, passionate audiences.

2. Lifestyle, Not Products: They sell belonging, identity, and transformation—not features.

3. Premium Positioning: Most charge more and compete on value/experience, not price.

4. Community & Belonging: Peloton, Lululemon, Airbnb—they build tribes, not customer bases.

5. Tech-Enabled Differentiation: Algorithms, platforms, data—technology creates defensible positioning.
